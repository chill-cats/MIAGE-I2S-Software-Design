\textbf{Design choices}: Each actor represents a role interacting with the system.
We chose to use a single Customer actor before and after signing in because
authentication changes the customer's access to the system without changing
their role. The Agency Employee actor represents an employee working at an
agency location who handles pickup and return activities. The Hotline Operator
actor handles incident reports and coordinates assistance following an incident.

These actors participate in the following use cases:

        \hline
        Pay & Customer\\
        \hline
        Cancel Reservation & Customer \\
        \hline
        Pickup Car & Customer, Agency Employee \\
        \hline
        Return Car & Customer, Agency Employee \\
        \hline
        Manage Incident & Customer, Hotline Operator \\
        \hline
        Report Incident & Customer \\
        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\caption{Use cases and their participating actors}
\end{table}
The \texttt{Make Reservation} use case covers selecting the pickup location, rental dates, car category, insurance and extra options. It also covers signing in and providing customer details before completing the booking. We keep these activities within one use case because they contribute to the same customer goal. We do not represent each form or selection as a separate use case.

We separate \texttt{Pay} from the other use cases because payment can occur during reservation, cancellation or pickup. We use conditional \emph{extend} relationships to show when payment is required. \texttt{Pay} extends \texttt{Make Reservation} when prepayment is selected. It extends \texttt{Cancel Reservation} when a prepaid reservation requires a cancellation fee. It extends \texttt{Pickup Car} when prepayment was not selected and the rental payment must be settled at the agency.
These extensions are conditional, but payment is required when the corresponding condition applies.

The \texttt{Cancel Reservation} use case allows the customer to cancel an existing booking. We assume that the customer must sign in and identify their reservation before cancellation. According to the assignment, cancelling a prepaid booking incurs a cancellation fee. The cancellation payment therefore represents this fee rather than another payment of the full rental price.

The \texttt{Pickup Car} use case involves both the customer and the agency employee. It covers finding the reservation, allocating a car from the booked category, checking and scanning the driving licence,
and recording the pickup inspection with the customer's digital signature. Any required rental payment must also be completed before the keys are handed over.
We keep these checks within the pickup use case and describe them in more detail through the system sequence diagram and low-fidelity interfaces.

The \texttt{Return Car} use case also involves the customer and the agency employee. The employee checks the car's condition, records the mileage and the return date and time, and obtains the customer's signature on the evaluation.
The car is returned to the pickup location, as required.
We do not model additional charges for damage, fuel or late returns because they are not mentioned in the assignment.

We separate \texttt{Report Incident} from \texttt{Manage Incident} because they have different goals.
Reporting requires the customer to provide the location, date, time and circumstances of an accident or breakdown, together with at least one photo.
Managing the incident covers the follow-up between the hotline operator and the customer to organise assistance.
This separation allows the report to be recorded before the hotline begins its response.

We treat signing in as part of the relevant use cases or as a precondition because authentication supports the rental process without constituting a separate business goal.

\chapter{Class Diagram}
\section{Design choices}

We have designed the class diagram to represent the main business entities of the car rental application. The classes follow the rental process: the customer chooses a car category and extra services, makes a reservation, pays either in advance or at pickup, picks up a car, and returns it after use. The diagram focuses on the business information and relationships. Interface components and controllers are not included in this high-level view.

The \texttt{Customer} class stores the customer's email address, first name and last name. We use a separate \texttt{Address} class to group the street, city, country and postal code. We assume that each customer has one recorded address, while several customers can share the same address. We also use \texttt{Address} to represent the pickup and return location instead of introducing a separate agency class. The customer's home address and the rental location are different objects of the same class.

The \texttt{DriverLicense} class stores a link to a scanned driving licence. A customer can have multiple recorded licences, each represented by a separate object. A customer may have no recorded licence when the reservation is made. The employee checks and scans the relevant licence during pickup.

We separate \texttt{CarType} from \texttt{Car} because the customer books a category before a specific vehicle is allocated. \texttt{CarType} describes the category through its name, description, typical numbers of passengers and windows, gearbox type and example car. \texttt{Car} stores the brand, colour and licence plate number of an actual vehicle. Each car belongs to one category, and one category can contain several cars.

We model \texttt{Reservation} as an abstract class with two concrete subclasses. The \texttt{PrepaidReservation} subclass represents prepaid bookings, while \texttt{PaylaterReservation} represents bookings paid at pickup. They share attributes but differ in behaviour, particularly when a reservation is cancelled.

We also separate \texttt{Reservation} from \texttt{Rental}. The reservation records the planned dates, payment option, status and agreed price. The rental records the actual pickup and return information, including the return mileage. A reservation can exist without a rental if the customer has not collected the car or has cancelled the booking. Each rental uses one specific car. The same car can be used for several rentals over time, but we assume that these rentals do not overlap.

The \texttt{Insurance} and \texttt{ExtraOption} classes describe the services associated with a reservation. Insurance has a daily price, while an extra option has a price and a picture. We keep these classes separate because their information and pricing are different. The same insurance or option can be selected in several reservations. Included insurance should not add an extra charge, although the current diagram does not explicitly distinguish included coverage from additional coverage.

The \texttt{Payment} class records each payment's amount, date, time and purpose. Payment options are represented by the \texttt{PaymentOption} enumeration, which distinguishes prepayment from payment at pickup. We do not create separate payment subclasses. Both choices use the same payment information; the main differences are the payment timing and pricing rules. A reservation can have several payments, for example, a rental payment followed by a cancellation fee. It can also have no recorded payment yet when payment at pickup is selected.

The \texttt{PickupVehicleInspection} and \texttt{ReturnVehicleInspection} classes record the car's condition and the customer's digital signature.
Each rental must have one pickup inspection and may have one return inspection, depending on its status. Both inspections must be signed before the rental is completed.
The signature can remain empty while an inspection is being prepared.

The \texttt{Incident} class records an accident or breakdown during a rental. It stores the location, date and time, and circumstances. Each incident must have at least one photo, represented by an \texttt{IncidentPhoto} object. A rental can have no incident or several incidents. We use enumerations for reservation status, payment option, payment purpose, inspection type, incident type and gearbox type to keep these values consistent.

We use composition between \texttt{Rental} and \texttt{Incident}, and between \texttt{Incident} and \texttt{IncidentPhoto}, to express exclusive ownership: each incident belongs to one rental, and each photo belongs to one incident. Their lifecycles depend on their respective owners. The other relationships use regular associations. We use \texttt{Integer} for counts and mileage, \texttt{Decimal} for monetary values, and \texttt{String} for licence plates and postal codes because they may contain letters or leading zeros.

In \texttt{Rental}, \texttt{returnDateTime} and \texttt{returnMileage} are optional because the actual return time and mileage are unknown until the car is returned.

\section{Reservation process}
\subsection{Overview}
The reservation process allows a customer to book a rental car for a selected location and period.
We divide the customer's journey into three stages.

% First, a user goes to the system and search the pickup location and the pickup and return dates.
% Using that information, the system presents available categories, their information on price, features
% and typical vehicle. The user can then select a category, choose any extra insurance and additional options,
% and then select either prepayment or payment at pickup. The system will clearly show that
% the included insurance if available carries no additional cost, and the prepayment price is lower than the
% pay-later price.

First, the customer describes their needs, and the system presents available offers to
help them choose a car category, insurance, extra options and a payment option.

Second, the customer signs in and provides their email, first name, last name and address.

Finally, the customer confirms the booking. If prepayment is selected, the customer pays by credit card,
and the payment is recorded in the system. A confirmed reservation is then created.

\subsection{Describe the reservation and calculate the price}
The customer provides the pickup location and rental dates to search for offers.
The \texttt{ReservationController} looks up available offers based on this information.
The customer then chooses a \texttt{CarType}.
The controller obtains the \texttt{CarType} details and presents the included insurance and available extras.
The customer then selects the desired extras.
For each selected additional insurance policy and extra option, the controller retrieves the price from the corresponding \texttt{Insurance} or \texttt{ExtraOption} object.
Once the customer chooses a payment option, the controller calculates the quote and returns a selection summary.

\subsection{Identify the customer}
The customer must sign in before completing the reservation. The controller authenticates the customer
and retrieves the corresponding \texttt{Customer} object.
After successful authentication, the customer provides their email, first name, last name and address.
The controller updates the customer's details and indicates that the booking is ready for confirmation.
The customer's address is distinct from the pickup location.
We decided not to include authentication infrastructure details in this diagram.

\subsection{Finalise the reservation}
The customer confirms that the booking details are correct. The controller rechecks availability and reserves capacity for the selected car category.
The \emph{alt} fragments distinguish between the two payment options.
In the case of prepayment, the controller creates a \texttt{Payment} object only after successful payment is verified.
For payment at pickup, no \texttt{Payment} object is created at this stage. The controller then creates a confirmed
\texttt{PrepaidReservation} or \texttt{PaylaterReservation}, depending on the payment option. The reservation contains the selected dates, category, location, insurance, extra options, payment option and quoted price.
For prepayment, the controller associates the payment with the reservation.
The resulting confirmation is returned to the controller and delivered to the customer, as shown in the overview diagram.
